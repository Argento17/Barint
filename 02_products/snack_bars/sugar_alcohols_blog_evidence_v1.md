## SECTION 1 — Polyol Energy Value: EU Regulatory Figure

**Claim to support:** A polyol gram costs less metabolic energy than a sucrose gram, which is why it lowers the calorie count on the label — but it is not zero.

### Finding

EU Regulation 1169/2011, Annex XIV (Conversion factors for energy calculation) assigns polyols a uniform energy value of:

> **10 kJ/g — 2.4 kcal/g**

This is the single figure used for all polyols on EU nutrition labels, regardless of the specific compound (maltitol, sorbitol, xylitol, erythritol, etc.). It contrasts with the 17 kJ/g — 4 kcal/g assigned to sugars and other carbohydrates under the same Annex.

**Important nuance:** The 2.4 kcal/g figure is a regulatory label convention, not a precise physiological measurement. Individual polyols differ at a biological level:
- Erythritol is almost entirely absorbed in the small intestine and excreted unchanged; its true caloric contribution is near 0.2 kcal/g (US FDA convention rounds it to 0 on American labels).
- Maltitol is partially absorbed and partially fermented; the EU label value (2.4 kcal/g) reasonably approximates its metabolisable energy.

For label purposes in the EU (and in Israeli labels that follow EU conventions), the single 2.4 kcal/g coefficient applies to all polyols.

**Evidence tier:** STRONG — direct from primary regulatory text.

**Sources:**
- Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 of the European Parliament and of the Council, Annex XIV, "Conversion factors for the calculation of energy value." EUR-Lex, 2011. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1169/oj/eng (UK retained version at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/1169/annex/XIV)
- European Association of Polyol Producers (EPA). "The Food Energy Value of Polyols." https://polyols-eu.org/legislation/food/ (cites the Reg. 1169/2011 Annex XIV values and notes per-compound variation from the uniform label coefficient — industry body, cited for the per-compound nuance only, not as a primary source)

**Practical framing note for Content Agent:** "EU labeling law assigns polyols 2.4 kcal/g — roughly 60% of the energy of sugar. On paper, swapping sucrose for maltitol therefore cuts both 'calories' and 'sugars' from a nutrition table simultaneously. That is the architectural trick."

## SECTION 2 — EU Mandatory Laxative Warning

**Claim to support:** EU law itself acknowledges that "excessive" polyol consumption has a physiological effect — manufacturers are legally required to warn about it.

### Finding

Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Annex III ("Mandatory particulars referred to in Article 21") requires:

> Foods with **more than 10% added polyols** must bear the mandatory statement:
> **"excessive consumption may produce laxative effects"**

This warning appears on the food packaging itself, not buried in ingredient lists.

**Threshold:** >10% added polyols by weight of the product.

**Evidence tier:** STRONG — direct from primary regulatory text. The wording is confirmed by multiple secondary regulatory-guidance sources including the European Commission's Health Knowledge Gateway and industry-facing guides (Cargill EU Labeling page; EPA Legislation page).

**Sources:**
- Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Annex III. EUR-Lex. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1169/oj/eng
- European Commission, Health Promotion Knowledge Gateway. "Sugars and Sweeteners." https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/health-promotion-knowledge-gateway/sugars-sweeteners_en — quotes: "Foods containing more than 10% added polyols...shall be labelled with the particular 'excessive consumption may produce laxative effects'."

**Practical framing note for Content Agent:** Do NOT frame this as "polyols are dangerous." Frame it as: "This is architectural evidence: EU lawmakers recognized the physiological reality of high polyol intake and built the warning into packaging law. A bar with 30+ g maltitol carries this warning. That is label-transparency; Bari surfaces what it means."

## SECTION 3 — Glycemic Response: Maltitol vs Sucrose

**Claim to support:** A bar's "low sugar" number does not mean low glycemic impact if the sugar was replaced with maltitol, because maltitol raises blood glucose — just less than sucrose.

### Finding

Maltitol produces a measurable postprandial glucose and insulin response. It is NOT glycemically neutral.

**Glycemic Index:**
The most-cited GI for crystalline maltitol is **35–36** (glucose reference = 100), compared to sucrose at approximately 65–69. Maltitol syrup (which contains higher-molecular-weight hydrogenated oligosaccharides) has a higher GI of approximately 52.

**Primary mechanistic evidence:**

Study 1 (RCT, n=8):
- Felber JP, Tappy L, Vouillamoz D, Randin JP, Jequier E. "Comparative study of maltitol and sucrose by means of continuous indirect calorimetry." *JPEN J Parenter Enteral Nutr.* 1987;11(3):250-254. DOI: 10.1177/0148607187011003250. PMID: 3298708.
- Design: 30 g oral load of maltitol vs. 30 g sucrose, 8 normal subjects, 6-hour monitoring.
- Key finding: Peak delta glucose was 21 ± 4 mg/100 ml after maltitol vs. 38 ± 4 mg/100 ml after sucrose (p < 0.02). Peak insulin was 9.3 ± 2.7 µU/ml (maltitol) vs. 25.5 ± 5.0 µU/ml (sucrose). Cumulated glucose oxidation with maltitol was 40% that of sucrose at 180 min.
- Limitation: Small n (8), 1987, acute single-dose design, healthy non-diabetic subjects only.
- Evidence tier for this study alone: WEAK (small n, single study, 1987 methods).

Study 2 (RCT, n=37):
- Remy C, Remy-Kristensen A, Gauthier M, Blanquet A. "Digestive tolerance and postprandial glycaemic and insulinaemic responses after consumption of dairy desserts containing maltitol and fructo-oligosaccharides in adults." *Eur J Clin Nutr.* 2014;68(5):575-580. DOI: 10.1038/ejcn.2014.30. PMID: 24569542. PMC4013556.
- This study confirmed lower glycaemic and insulinaemic response vs. sucrose comparator, while also assessing digestive tolerance at realistic food doses.
- Evidence tier: MODERATE (larger n, RCT, realistic food vehicle, but single study).

**EFSA position:** EFSA's 2011 Scientific Opinion (NDA Panel) assessed health claims for sugar replacers including maltitol and concluded that "consumption of foods/drinks containing [sugar replacers such as maltitol] instead of sugar induces a lower blood glucose rise after their consumption compared with sugar-containing foods/drinks." EFSA approved this as a substantiated claim.
- Source: EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA). "Scientific Opinion on the substantiation of health claims related to the sugar replacers..." *EFSA Journal.* 2011;9(4):2076. DOI: 10.2903/j.efsa.2011.2076. https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.2903/j.efsa.2011.2076

**Overall evidence tier:** MODERATE — Multiple RCTs plus EFSA regulatory opinion confirm lower (but not zero) glycemic response. Contested at the margins for diabetic populations and for maltitol syrup vs. crystalline forms.

**[HEALTH-CLAIM RISK NOTE]** The statement "maltitol raises blood glucose" is accurate and defensible. Do NOT extend it to "therefore diabetics should avoid it" — that is medical advice and outside Bari's lane. Frame as: "The low 'sugar' figure on the label does not equal zero glycemic impact. The architecture matters."

## SECTION 4 — Gastrointestinal Tolerance and Dose Thresholds

**Claim to support:** Polyol-heavy bars carry a physiological consequence (GI symptoms) that is dose-dependent and not visible from the nutrition panel. This is an architectural transparency issue.

### Finding

Polyol GI effects operate via two mechanisms: (a) incomplete absorption in the small intestine, drawing water into the gut osmotically; (b) bacterial fermentation in the large intestine, producing gas and short-chain fatty acids. Both are dose-dependent.

**Systematic review (best available evidence):**
- Lenhart A, Chey WD. "A Systematic Review of the Effects of Polyols on Gastrointestinal Health and Irritable Bowel Syndrome." *Advances in Nutrition.* 2017;8(4):587-596. DOI: 10.3945/an.117.015560. PMID: 28710145. PMC5508768.
- 79 papers included from 1823 screened.
- Key conclusion: "Polyol malabsorption generally occurs in a dose-dependent fashion in healthy individuals, and malabsorption increases when polyols are ingested in combination... Polyols can induce dose-dependent symptoms of flatulence, abdominal discomfort, and laxative effects when consumed by both healthy volunteers and patients with IBS."
- Dose thresholds for sorbitol (the most studied individual polyol): 10 g/day = mild symptoms (flatulence/bloating) in healthy adults; 20 g/day = more distressing symptoms including abdominal pain and diarrhea.
- For maltitol: dose-response confirmed but specific per-serving thresholds less-studied than sorbitol; reviewed studies note similar mechanism class.
- IBS note: "the dose-response curve appears to be shifted to the left" in IBS — symptoms at lower doses.
- Evidence tier: STRONG for the existence and dose-dependence of GI effects; MODERATE for specific maltitol thresholds.

**Maltitol-specific controlled trial:**
- Ruskone-Fourmestraux A, Attar A, Chassard D, Coffin B, Bornet F, Bouhnik Y. "A digestive tolerance study of maltitol after occasional and regular consumption in healthy humans." *Eur J Clin Nutr.* 2003;57(4):592-595. DOI: 10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601516. PMID: 12548293.
- Design: Double-blind RCT, n=12 healthy volunteers. Escalating daily doses until diarrhea or grade-3 GI symptom occurred (= "threshold dose"). Two periods: occasional (once/week) and regular (daily for 9 days).
- Key finding: Mean threshold dose for diarrhea/severe symptoms was **92 ± 6 g/day (occasional) and 93 ± 9 g/day (regular)** — i.e., tolerance was similar regardless of adaptation period. However, diarrhea appeared in 6/12 subjects (occasional) and 8/12 (regular) — higher frequency than sucrose controls.
- Conclusion from the authors: At doses "much greater than those currently used" in food products, maltitol causes diarrhea. At realistic single-bar doses, severe symptoms are unlikely in healthy adults.
- Limitation: n=12, single lab, acute escalation protocol, healthy adults only.
- Evidence tier: MODERATE (RCT but small n; findings limited to healthy adults without IBS).

**Context for a protein bar:** A typical protein bar contains 15–30 g maltitol per bar (100 g bar at 15–30% polyol content is common in the Israeli market based on the Bari corpus ingredient review). This is below the 92 g diarrhea threshold for healthy adults — but it is above the ~10–20 g range where mild GI symptoms (bloating, flatulence) begin in sensitive individuals, particularly if multiple bars are consumed.

**[HEALTH-CLAIM RISK NOTE]** State: "At typical single-bar amounts, most healthy adults will not experience severe effects. Sensitive individuals and those with IBS may experience mild symptoms at lower doses." Do NOT state "this will make you sick." Cite the Lenhart/Chey 2017 systematic review, not the Ruskone-Fourmestraux dose escalation (which used unrealistically high doses to find a threshold).

**Notes on the table:**
1. EU label energy (2.4 kcal/g) is the same for all four under Regulation 1169/2011 Annex XIV. This is the figure that appears on Israeli and EU nutrition panels.

4. Digestive tolerance data: erythritol's low GI symptom profile is why the manufacturer applied to EFSA for an exemption from the mandatory laxative warning (EFSA 2023 opinion).

**Evidence tier for the table:** MODERATE overall. GI values are from small/older RCTs or derived from regulatory opinion; broad consensus in the literature is consistent. No single systematic meta-analysis provides a head-to-head table with all four compounds in one study.

## SECTION 6 — Erythritol 2023 Cardiovascular Signal (Hazen et al.) — CAVEATED CONTEXT ONLY

**Purpose:** This section exists so the Content Agent and Adversarial QA can make an informed decision about whether to mention erythritol's cardiovascular signal in the article. It is NOT safe to publish without the full caveats. Recommendation: omit or include only with the framing below.

### The primary finding

- Witkowski M, Nemet I, Alamri H, et al. "The artificial sweetener erythritol and cardiovascular event risk." *Nature Medicine.* 2023;29(3):710-718. DOI: 10.1038/s41591-023-02223-9. PMID: 36849732.
- Multi-method study: untargeted metabolomics in a discovery cohort (n=1,157), validated in two independent cohorts (US n=2,149; European n=833), plus in-vitro platelet experiments and a murine thrombosis model.
- Finding: High circulating plasma erythritol was associated with ~1.8–2.2× increased 3-year risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients already undergoing cardiac risk assessment. In vitro, erythritol enhanced platelet aggregation and adhesion.

### A 2024 follow-up human interventional study

- Witkowski M et al. (Hazen lab). "Ingestion of the Non-Nutritive Sweetener Erythritol, but Not Glucose, Enhances Platelet Reactivity and Thrombosis Potential in Healthy Volunteers — Brief Report." *Arterioscler Thromb Vasc Biol.* 2024. DOI: 10.1161/ATVBAHA.124.321019. PMID: 39114916.
- n=10 per group, prospective interventional. 30 g erythritol produced a >1,000-fold increase in plasma erythritol and enhanced platelet reactivity vs. 30 g glucose comparator in healthy volunteers.

### The caveats — substantial and published

**Confounding and reverse causality (key counter-argument):**

Cramer T, Gonder U, Kofler B. "Plasma erythritol and cardiovascular risk: is there evidence for an association with dietary intake?" *Front Nutr.* 2023;10:1195521. DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2023.1195521. PMID (PMC10242034).
- The authors note elevated plasma erythritol may reflect pentose phosphate pathway dysregulation from high-glucose/high-fructose diets — erythritol is produced endogenously as well as from dietary intake.
- The discovery cohorts comprised patients already undergoing cardiac risk assessment; they had higher average age (10–14 years older) and higher diabetes prevalence than low-erythritol groups.
- Conclusion: "No causal relationship between erythritol consumption and cardiovascular risk can be derived from the epidemiological study results."

**Independent expert reaction at time of publication (Science Media Centre, 2023):**
- Prof. Gunter Kuhnle (Univ. of Reading): The in vitro experimental dose was "10-fold higher than the permitted amount in drinks" and "more than most of us would eat during an entire day."
- Prof. Nita Forouhi (MRC Epidemiology, Cambridge): Important confounders unaccounted for — "ethnicity, socioeconomic status, alcohol consumption, overall diet." Cohorts "undergoing cardiac risk assessment not in healthy or general populations."
- Dr. Simon Mellor (ADA): Erythritol can be manufactured inside the human body (pentose phosphate pathway, elevated in high-glucose states) — so "plasma erythritol" may reflect lifestyle, not sweetener intake. Source: Science Media Centre expert reactions, February 2023. https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-study-looking-at-an-artificial-sweetener-erythritol-and-cardiovascular-disease-events/

**EFSA 2023 regulatory conclusion:**
- EFSA NDA Panel. "Re-evaluation of erythritol (E 968) as a food additive." *EFSA Journal.* 2023;21(12):e8430. DOI: 10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8430.
- EFSA concluded: "The available evidence does not demonstrate a causal relationship between dietary intake of erythritol (E 968) as a food additive and increased risk for cardiovascular disease and related risk factors."
- EFSA noted: "Further research could be useful to clarify the nature of the association found in some observational studies."

**Evidence tier for the cardiovascular signal:** CONTESTED. The observational association is replicated across three cohorts (Hazen et al. 2023). The 2024 interventional study adds a platelet mechanism in healthy subjects. But causality is disputed: EFSA's 2023 formal assessment found no causal link from dietary intake; independent experts raised substantial confounding concerns; endogenous production confounds plasma-level studies; in-vitro doses were supraphysiological. This is an active area with genuine scientific uncertainty.

### Recommendation for Content Agent

This section is **FLAGGED** for the Content Agent / Adversarial QA gate.

**Do NOT include as a settled fact.** If you mention erythritol at all in the article:
- Frame only as: "An observational study (Witkowski et al., Nature Medicine 2023) found an association between high plasma erythritol and cardiovascular events. Independent experts and EFSA (2023) note this does not establish causality from dietary erythritol, and endogenous production of erythritol complicates interpretation."
- Do NOT say "erythritol causes heart attacks."
- The architectural point for Bari is: even erythritol — often presented as the "cleanest" polyol — has an unresolved scientific debate around it, which is why Bari surfaces ingredient architecture rather than making health recommendations.

## SECTION 7 — Israeli Label Context

**Claim to support:** What does a protein bar's nutrition panel in Israel show about polyols? And does Israeli law carry an equivalent laxative warning?

### Finding

**Partial — verified with confidence limitation stated.**

**What is verified:**

1. **Polyols listed under carbohydrates in Israeli panels:** Israel's nutrition labeling regulations (updated 2020) require the nutrition table to specify carbohydrate content with a sub-breakdown including sugars and polyalcohols (כוהלי סוכר / רב-כהלים). This aligns with EU convention. Source: USDA Foreign Agricultural Service, GAIN Report "New Nutritional Labeling Regulation — Israel," January 2018; and USDA GAIN Country Report, December 2019. (Note: these documents' PDF versions were not fully readable in this research session — see methodology note below.)

2. **Mandatory laxative warning in Israeli law:** Multiple secondary sources state that Israeli food regulations require: products in which polyols have been added and account for more than 10% of the packaged food's weight must display the text: **"Excessive consumption may lead to intestine dysmotility"** (Hebrew wording not confirmed independently in this session). The Israeli warning wording differs slightly from the EU's "may produce laxative effects" but covers the same mechanism. Source: USDA GAIN Report summary language (2018/2019 reports); secondary regulatory guidance consulted in this research.

3. **Regulatory basis:** The Israeli sweetener labeling warning is cited as originating from the Protection of Public Health (Food) (Labeling of Products Containing Certain Sweeteners) Regulations, 2018 (Hebrew regulation). The primary Hebrew-language text was not directly accessed in this session.

**What is NOT verified to primary-source standard:**
- The exact Hebrew wording of the Israeli laxative-equivalent warning on-pack.
- Whether Israeli regulations use the term "כוהלי סוכר" (sugar alcohols) or "רב-כהלים" (polyols) as the primary Hebrew label term — both appear in secondary sources; the official term in the current Israeli standard needs confirmation from the Israeli Standards Institute (SII, מכון התקנים הישראלי) or MoH primary text.
- Whether Israeli law distinguishes erythritol from other polyols for the warning threshold.

**Methodology note on Israeli primary sources:** The USDA GAIN PDF reports for Israel were downloaded but could not be text-extracted by the research tools in this session (binary PDF encoding). The Israeli MoH Efsharibari website was referenced in searches but the specific polyol-warning text was not extracted from a primary Hebrew legislative document. This limits the Israeli section to MODERATE confidence.

**Evidence tier:** MODERATE for the existence of a polyol warning requirement in Israeli law; INSUFFICIENT for exact Hebrew wording and term from primary Israeli sources.

**Recommendation for Content Agent / Adversarial QA:** If the article includes a statement about Israeli labeling, restrict it to:
- "Israeli labeling regulations, which align with EU convention, require polyols to be listed under carbohydrates on the nutrition panel."
- If citing the laxative warning: "Israeli regulations require a warning for products where polyols exceed 10% by weight — broadly equivalent to the EU's requirement."
- Do NOT state the exact Hebrew wording without a Nutrition Agent or Israeli regulatory specialist confirming the primary Ministry of Health text.
