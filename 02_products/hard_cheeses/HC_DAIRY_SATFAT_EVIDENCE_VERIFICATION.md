## WHAT THE RULE PREMISE IS ACTUALLY CLAIMING

The claim the rule rests on: "fermented hard-cheese saturated fat attenuates LDL-cholesterol
response relative to butter at equal saturated fat intake, owing to the cheese food matrix."

This is a narrower claim than "cheese sat-fat is benign" — it says specifically:
1. When saturated fat is consumed IN cheese form, the LDL elevation is less than when the
   same grams of sat-fat are consumed as butter.
2. The mechanism is the cheese food matrix (calcium, protein structure, milk fat globule
   membrane, fermentation products).

## VERIFIED EVIDENCE BASE: REAL PAPERS ON THE CHEESE MATRIX / LDL QUESTION

### VERIFIED PAPER A — Brassard et al. 2017, AJCN

**Full citation:** Brassard D, Tessier-Grenier M, Allaire J, et al. "Comparison of the
impact of SFAs from cheese and butter on cardiometabolic risk factors: a randomized
controlled trial." *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition.* 2017;105(4):800–809.
DOI: 10.3945/ajcn.116.150300. PMID: 28251937.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28251937/

**Design:** Multicenter, crossover, randomized controlled trial.

**Sample:** 92 adults with abdominal obesity and low HDL-cholesterol.

**Arms:** 5 dietary periods (4 weeks each, 4-week washout): (1) high-SFA cheese (~12.4%
energy), (2) high-SFA butter (~12.6% energy), (3) MUFA-rich, (4) PUFA-rich, (5) low-fat
high-carbohydrate. Both cheese and butter arms delivered matched total fat and saturated fat.

**Duration:** 4 weeks per arm.

**LDL finding (direction, effect size, significance):**
- Cheese diet LDL was LOWER than butter diet by approximately 3.3% (p < 0.05).
- Cheese diet LDL was HIGHER than carbohydrate, MUFA, and PUFA diets (+2.6%, +5.3%,
  +12.3%, p < 0.05 for all comparisons).
- The LDL response was moderated by baseline: the cheese vs. butter difference was
  significant only among participants with high baseline LDL (p-interaction = 0.02).

**Comparison basis:** Cheese vs. BUTTER at equal saturated fat — this directly tests the
matrix-attenuation claim. Cheese sat-fat raises LDL less than butter sat-fat. However, both
high-SFA diets (cheese AND butter) raised LDL relative to lower-SFA alternatives.

**Limitations:**
- High-SFA population (abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia) — generalizability to healthy
  adults or typical Israeli cheese consumers unclear.
- Crossover design with 4-week washout: carry-over effects possible.
- 4-week dietary period is short; steady-state lipid effects may not be reached.
- LDL is a surrogate marker; no hard CV outcome data.
- The attenuation (3.3%) is modest and was effect-modified by baseline — not universal.
- Funding: "Research Support, Non-U.S. Gov't" — full disclosure not visible at abstract
  level; dairy industry funding cannot be excluded.

**Verdict:** PARTIALLY SUPPORTS the matrix-attenuation premise. Cheese sat-fat raises LDL
LESS than butter sat-fat at equal intake, but BOTH raise LDL vs. lower-SFA diets.
The effect is real but modest and was concentrated in individuals with high baseline LDL.

**Evidence Tier:** Moderate. One RCT with a specific population, modest effect size,
short duration, and baseline-modifier interaction.

### VERIFIED PAPER B — Feeney et al. 2018, AJCN

**Full citation:** Feeney EL, Barron R, Dible V, et al. "Dairy matrix effects: response to
consumption of dairy fat differs when eaten within the cheese matrix — a randomized
controlled trial." *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition.* 2018;108(4):667–674.
DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/nqy146. PMID: 30107488.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30107488/

**Design:** Randomized parallel intervention.

**Sample:** 164 volunteers (75 men), aged ≥50, overweight.

**Arms:** ~40g dairy fat daily for 6 weeks in four forms:
(A) 120g full-fat Irish cheddar (n=46),
(B) 120g reduced-fat cheddar + 21g butter (n=45),
(C) 49g butter + 30g calcium caseinate + CaCO₃ supplement (n=42),
(D) 120g full-fat cheddar after 6-week cheese-exclusion run-in (n=31).

**Duration:** 6 weeks.

**LDL finding:**
- Full cheese matrix (Group A) vs. deconstructed components (Group C):
  LDL: 2.97 ± 0.67 mmol/L (cheese) vs. 3.43 ± 0.78 mmol/L (butter+components), p=0.026.
  Total cholesterol: 5.23 ± 0.88 vs. 5.57 ± 0.86 mmol/L, p=0.033.
- Stepwise matrix effect confirmed: the more intact the cheese structure, the lower the LDL.

**Comparison basis:** Cheese matrix vs. equivalent fat in deconstructed form (butter +
casein powder + calcium). This is a particularly clean test of the matrix hypothesis because
it isolates the structural/matrix variable from the nutrient composition variable.

**Limitations:**
- Overweight adults aged ≥50 (not a general population sample; results may not generalize
  to younger or leaner individuals).
- Parallel design (not crossover) — between-arm baseline differences possible.
- Irish cheddar specifically — hard cheese type may matter; Israeli cheeses (yellow,
  Gouda, Emmental) may differ in calcium content, fermentation profile, fat structure.
- 6 weeks is short for lipid endpoint studies.
- LDL is a surrogate marker; no hard outcomes.
- Funding/COI: not disclosed in abstract; lead author group (Gibney/Feeney) have received
  Food for Health Ireland / Enterprise Ireland funding including dairy industry co-funding
  (confirmed in the 2022 secondary analysis by O'Connor et al., Frontiers in Nutrition,
  DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2022.945723, PMID 35990333).

**Verdict:** SUPPORTS the matrix-attenuation premise for full-fat cheese vs. butter.
Effect size is clinically meaningful (LDL difference ~0.46 mmol/L). Study design is
well-controlled. Concern: cheese type generalizability and COI in the author group.

**Evidence Tier:** Moderate-Strong for the matrix-vs-butter comparison within this specific
population and cheese type.

### VERIFIED PAPER C — Hjerpsted, Leedo & Tholstrup 2011, AJCN

**Full citation:** Hjerpsted J, Leedo E, Tholstrup T. "Cheese intake in large amounts lowers
LDL-cholesterol concentrations compared with butter intake of equal fat content."
*American Journal of Clinical Nutrition.* 2011;94(6):1479–1484.
DOI: 10.3945/ajcn.111.022426. PMID: 22030228.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22030228/

**Design:** Randomized crossover trial.

**Sample:** 49 men and women.

**Arms:** Hard cheese providing 13% energy from fat vs. butter providing 13% energy from fat,
each for 6 weeks.

**LDL finding:** Cheese vs. butter: cheese resulted in LOWER total and LDL-cholesterol.
Critically, cheese did NOT raise LDL vs. baseline habitual diet, despite higher sat-fat.
Specific effect sizes/p-values not available at abstract level; full text not retrieved.

**Comparison basis:** Hard cheese vs. butter at equal total fat — direct test of matrix claim.

**Limitations:**
- Small sample (n=49), short duration (6 weeks).
- LDL is a surrogate marker.
- Full text not available for this verification; abstract-level only for effect sizes.
- COI not disclosed in available excerpt.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTS the matrix-attenuation premise. This is the earliest clean RCT showing
cheese vs. butter LDL difference at equal fat intake.

**Evidence Tier:** Moderate (small n, older study, abstract-level for effect sizes here).

### VERIFIED PAPER D — Pradeilles et al. 2023, Advances in Nutrition (Meta-Analysis)

**Full citation:** Pradeilles R, Norris T, Sellem L, Markey O. "Effect of Isoenergetic
Substitution of Cheese with Other Dairy Products on Blood Lipid Markers in the Fasted and
Postprandial State: An Updated and Extended Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of
Randomized Controlled Trials in Adults." *Advances in Nutrition.* 2023.
DOI: 10.1016/j.advnut.2023.09.003. PMID: 37717700.
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10721513/

**Design:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs.

**Trials included:** 7 RCTs in fasting meta-analysis (264 participants), plus 3 postprandial
studies in narrative synthesis (10 articles total).

**LDL finding:** Cheese vs. butter comparison: cheese reduced fasting LDL-C by 0.19 mmol/L
(cited as "of public health importance" given that 1.0 mmol/L LDL reduction associates
with ~19% lower coronary mortality risk). Moderate-to-substantial statistical heterogeneity
noted. Cheese vs. milk comparison was data-sparse (only 2 studies, n=29).

**Comparison basis:** Cheese vs. butter — direct meta-analytic test of the matrix claim.

**Limitations:**
- Only 3 databases searched; no grey literature.
- Substantial heterogeneity across trials.
- Only 264 participants across 7 trials — small aggregate.
- Effect size (0.19 mmol/L) is modest in absolute terms (~1/5 of a clinically important
  reduction).
- Postprandial evidence "very uncertain."
- Typical trial cheese quantities (135g/day) exceed typical Israeli consumer intake.
- One co-author (Markey) received funding from Arla Food Ingredients — partial dairy
  industry tie.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTS the matrix-attenuation premise at the meta-analytic level. This is
the strongest evidence tier available for the specific cheese-vs-butter comparison.

**Evidence Tier:** Moderate. Meta-analysis of small RCTs with heterogeneity and partial
COI; consistent direction but modest effect size.

### VERIFIED PAPER E — Lordan, Tsoupras, Mitra & Zabetakis 2018, Foods

**Full citation:** Lordan R, Tsoupras A, Mitra B, Zabetakis I. "Dairy Fats and Cardiovascular
Disease: Do We Really Need to Be Concerned?" *Foods.* 2018;7(3):29.
DOI: 10.3390/foods7030029. PMID: 29494487.
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867544/

NOTE: This is the REAL Lordan/Tsoupras/Zabetakis dairy-and-CVD paper. It was published in
*Foods* (not *Nutrients*) in 2018 (not 2019), and carries PMID 29494487 (not 31022985).
The specification's cited paper (PMID 31022985, "Lordan 2019 Nutrients") does not exist.

**Design:** Narrative review.

**Key findings:** Cheese consumption does not significantly raise LDL in clinical studies;
in contrast, whole milk and butter raise LDL similarly. The dairy food matrix (calcium
saponification of saturated fatty acids, milk fat globule membrane phospholipids, fermented
bioactives) is proposed as the mechanism. Authors note the evidence is supportive but
mechanistic confirmation in humans remains incomplete.

**Limitations:**
- Narrative review — susceptible to selection bias.
- Mechanistic claims (calcium soap formation) are based on in vitro and animal data, not
  confirmed directly in human cheeses of varied type.
- Does not establish that ALL hard cheese types show attenuation equally.
- Does not distinguish fermented vs. unfermented hard cheese.

**Verdict:** PARTIALLY SUPPORTS the matrix-attenuation premise. Provides mechanistic
context and summary of observational + trial evidence, but is a narrative review, not
original trial data or meta-analysis.

**Evidence Tier:** Weak (narrative review, non-systematic, mechanism partly in vitro).

## COUNTER-EVIDENCE

### Counter-1: Cheese still raises LDL relative to lower-SFA diets

Brassard et al. 2017 (verified above, PMID 28251937) found that both cheese AND butter diets
raised LDL-cholesterol significantly vs. carbohydrate, MUFA, and PUFA diets. The
matrix-attenuation claim is specifically cheese-vs-butter; it does NOT mean cheese sat-fat
is benign vs. lower-fat alternatives. Consumer copy that implies "cheese sat-fat is fine"
would misstate the evidence.

### Counter-2: Matrix effect may not extend to all hard cheese types

Feeney et al. 2018 used Irish cheddar specifically. Rooney et al. 2025 (PMID 39923541,
Atherosclerosis, "The impact of sex and the cheese matrix on cholesterol metabolism in
middle-aged adults") found sex-differential responses (women benefit more than men from
intact cheese vs. deconstructed cheese). The matrix effect is not universal across
populations or cheese types.

Israeli market hard cheeses (yellow cheese, Gouda, Emmental, processed slices) vary
considerably in fermentation depth, calcium content, and MFGM integrity — the key
matrix-attenuation factors. No Israeli-market-specific evidence was found.

### Counter-3: LDL as surrogate — does not map to hard CV outcomes

Givens 2022 (PMID 36285545, *Nutrition Bulletin*, "Saturated fats, dairy foods and
cardiovascular health: No longer a curious paradox?") argues forcefully that LDL-C as a
predictor would "lead to a completely wrong prediction of CVD-related risk" for dairy foods,
because prospective cohort studies consistently show neutral or inverse associations between
cheese/dairy intake and CVD events — despite LDL raising in some arms.

Prospective meta-analyses (Consumption of Dairy Foods and Cardiovascular Disease, PMC
8875110; Dairy Product Consumption and CVD, PMC 8970833) confirm that cheese at typical
intake is neutrally or inversely associated with CHD risk (RR ~0.96 per 20g/day higher
intake) — supporting the broader claim that cheese sat-fat is metabolically different from
other sat-fat sources, but this evidence comes from observational data not RCTs.

### Counter-4: Raziani et al. 2016, AJCN — Regular-fat cheese does NOT increase LDL vs. reduced-fat cheese

Raziani F, et al. "High intake of regular-fat cheese compared with reduced-fat cheese does
not affect LDL cholesterol or risk markers of the metabolic syndrome: a randomized
controlled trial." *AJCN.* 2016;104(4):973–981. PMID: 27557654.

In 139 completers over 12 weeks, regular-fat vs. reduced-fat cheese showed NO significant
LDL difference. This is consistent with attenuation but also highlights that the
inter-cheese sat-fat effect is so attenuated as to be undetectable — meaning the marginal
LDL impact of full-fat vs. reduced-fat cheese, within the cheese matrix, is negligible.
This does NOT mean cheese raises LDL; it means the LDL penalty for choosing regular over
reduced-fat cheese is smaller than for butter equivalents.

### Counter-5: Baseline-modifier interaction — effect not uniform

Brassard et al. 2017 found the cheese vs. butter LDL difference was significant ONLY in
individuals with high baseline LDL (p-interaction = 0.02). In normal-LDL individuals, the
cheese vs. butter advantage was not statistically significant. This limits generalizability
to an Israeli consumer population that includes people with variable baseline lipid profiles.

## NET EVIDENCE-STRENGTH VERDICT

**Specific claim being evaluated:** "Fermented hard-cheese saturated fat deserves LESS binary-cap
severity than engineered reformulable saturated fat" (i.e., the cheese-vs-butter
matrix attenuation claim, NOT the claim that cheese sat-fat is benign overall).

**Verdict: MODERATE**

Rationale:
- The cheese-vs-butter LDL difference is consistent across 3 RCTs (Hjerpsted 2011, Brassard
  2017, Feeney 2018) and confirmed in a 2023 meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (Pradeilles 2023).
  The direction is unambiguous: cheese sat-fat raises LDL less than butter sat-fat at equal
  intake. This is the core attenuation claim and it holds.
- However: effect sizes are modest (0.19 mmol/L meta-analytic LDL reduction vs. butter);
  all trials are short (4–12 weeks); total sample across trials is small; heterogeneity is
  substantial; and the effect is baseline-modified.
- The cheese types studied are Western (cheddar, generic hard cheese) — not Israeli-market
  cheeses specifically. The mechanism (calcium saponification, MFGM) is plausible but
  not chemically confirmed in all cheese types.
- LDL is a surrogate marker. Hard CV outcome data do not exist at the trial level, though
  prospective cohort meta-analyses support neutral/protective associations for cheese.
- Funding/COI is a recurring concern across multiple trials (dairy-adjacent funding in
  Feeney/Gibney group; Markey/Arla in Pradeilles 2023; Astrup disclosures in Astrup 2019).

NOT Strong because: no systematic review with pooled effect estimates from high-quality
fully-powered trials; surrogate endpoint only; cheese-type and population heterogeneity.

NOT Weak because: consistent directional evidence across multiple independent RCTs; plausible
and partially confirmed mechanism; consistent meta-analytic signal.

## CONSUMER COPY GUIDANCE

### What CAN be said (evidence-warranted):

1. When saturated fat is consumed in whole cheese form rather than as butter, the rise in
   LDL-cholesterol is measurably smaller, according to multiple randomized trials and a
   2023 meta-analysis. This is an accurate, evidence-grounded claim.

2. Studies comparing equivalent amounts of cheese and butter consistently find cheese
   produces a less pronounced LDL response — an effect attributed to the food matrix
   (calcium, protein, structure) rather than the fat content itself.

3. Hard cheese occupies a different position in the evidence landscape than butter or
   processed spreads with comparable saturated fat — the binary sat-fat cap, designed for
   reformulable fats, applies less cleanly to a whole-food fermented matrix.

### What CANNOT be said (overstates the evidence):

1. "Cheese sat-fat does not raise LDL" — FALSE. Both the Brassard RCT and the
   Pradeilles meta-analysis show cheese DOES raise LDL vs. lower-SFA diets (MUFA, PUFA,
   carbohydrate). The attenuation is vs. butter, not vs. all alternatives.

2. "Cheese saturated fat is safe for cardiovascular health" — UNWARRANTED at RCT level.
   Long-term hard outcome data are from observational cohorts (not trials) and are
   confounded by healthy user bias, dietary displacement, etc.

3. "Studies show cheese protects against heart disease" — MISLEADING. Cohort associations
   are neutral-to-inverse but do not establish causation.

4. Do NOT name or cite the three original PMIDs (39133879, 28615384, 31022985) in any
   consumer or internal copy — all three refer to completely unrelated papers.

5. "Multiple clinical studies confirm that cheese fat behaves differently from other
   saturated fats" — technically defensible but requires careful framing; "clinical studies"
   with short duration in specialized populations must not be presented as definitive proof.

### Framing guidance for the Bari scoring rule rationale:

The rule can rest on: cheese-vs-butter matrix-attenuation effect, confirmed across 3 RCTs
and 1 meta-analysis, MODERATE evidence tier. It should be scoped to: clean fermented hard
cheese (not processed cheese slices, not cheese spreads). Consumer copy should say what the
evidence shows (less LDL elevation vs. butter at equal sat-fat) and NOT what it does not
show (no LDL effect, CV safety guarantee, dose-response linearity).

## OPEN QUESTIONS FOR NUTRITION AGENT

1. Does the rule scope to fermented hard cheese only? Processed cheese slices and cheese
   spreads are NOT covered by the matrix-attenuation evidence and should remain under the
   standard sat-fat cap.

2. What cheese sat-fat threshold triggers the rule? The evidence tested ~13% energy from
   fat (Hjerpsted 2011), ~12% SFA from cheese (Brassard 2017), and ~40g fat/day (Feeney
   2018). Israeli hard cheese per-100g typically contains 25–30g fat, of which 16–20g is
   saturated. A per-serving threshold needs anchoring.

3. Israeli market generalizability: The trials used Irish cheddar (Feeney), Canadian
   cheeses (Brassard), and Danish hard cheeses (Hjerpsted). Israeli yellow cheese, Gouda,
   and Emmental have different fat structures and fermentation profiles. No Israeli-specific
   RCT evidence exists. This is a gap the rule must note.
