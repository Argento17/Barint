## THE PROMPT (copy everything between the lines below into your research tool)

---

I'm researching creatine (the sports-nutrition supplement, creatine monohydrate and related forms) to build a consumer-facing product comparison and an explainer article. I need a rigorous, citation-backed research report. Please follow these instructions exactly.

**Ground rules for every section below:**
- Cite a real, checkable source for every factual claim — a PMID, a DOI, or a named authoritative body (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, EFSA, ISSN — International Society of Sports Nutrition — position stand, a named peer-reviewed journal article with authors and year). If you are not confident a citation is real, say so explicitly rather than presenting it as fact.
- Explicitly separate **strong evidence** (meta-analyses, systematic reviews, multiple consistent RCTs) from **weaker or contested evidence** (single small studies, animal/in-vitro only, mechanistic reasoning without human outcome data, industry-funded studies without independent replication).
- Note any **conflict of interest** in a source — for example, a study funded by a creatine manufacturer, or an author with a financial tie to a supplement brand.
- Do **not** use Open Food Facts (a crowdsourced food database) as a source for anything in this report — it is not permitted for this project.
- Where evidence is genuinely mixed, unsettled, or thin, say so plainly rather than rounding up to a confident answer.

### 1. Efficacy evidence base, by claim

For each of the following claimed benefits, tell me: what the evidence actually shows, the strength/quality of that evidence (systematic review/meta-analysis vs. isolated RCT vs. weak/preliminary), and the citations.

- Strength and power output (with resistance training)
- Lean muscle mass gain (with resistance training)
- Exercise performance in high-intensity, repeated-effort activities (sprinting, HIIT)
- Recovery from exercise (reduced muscle damage markers, faster return to training)
- Cognitive function / brain health (especially in sleep-deprived, vegetarian/vegan, or older-adult populations — this is an area of active but younger research)
- Any other claim you see commonly marketed (e.g., fat loss, "energy," anti-aging, mood) — tell me plainly which of these have real evidence and which are marketing claims with no matching studied endpoint.

I already have one internal reference point: an existing evidence review rated "strength/lean mass with resistance training" as **Strong** (multiple consistent meta-analyses) and "fat loss/fat burning" as **Insufficient** (no credible evidence trail). Please independently verify or challenge this — don't just confirm it, actually check it — and then go deeper on the claims I haven't already rated (especially cognitive/recovery), since those are the gap I need filled.

### 2. Effective dose ranges, by goal

- What is the standard **loading protocol** (dose, frequency, duration) and is loading actually necessary, or is it just a faster on-ramp to the same steady-state result?
- What is the **maintenance dose** range — minimum effective, typical, and the upper end actually studied?
- Does the effective dose differ meaningfully by body weight, sex, training status, or by which claimed outcome you're targeting (e.g., does the cognitive-benefit literature use a different dose than the strength literature)?
- Is there a dose below which creatine is present on a label but physiologically unlikely to do anything ("decorative" amount)? What's the literature basis for that floor?

### 3. Forms and bioavailability

- Creatine monohydrate vs. creatine HCl vs. buffered/"alkaline" creatine (e.g. Kre-Alkalyn) vs. creatine ethyl ester vs. creatine citrate/malate vs. any other commercially sold form — for each, is there actual human evidence of superiority (absorption, efficacy, tolerability) over monohydrate, or is superiority a marketing claim without a matching study?
- Solubility and stability differences between forms.
- **Specifically important for my use case:** creatine's chemical stability when dissolved in a liquid dairy matrix (e.g., a milk-based or yogurt-based protein drink) over the product's shelf life — does creatine degrade to creatinine over time in a liquid/dairy environment, at what rate, under what pH/temperature conditions, and is this a known formulation challenge for functional dairy beverages? Please look specifically for food-science / formulation literature on this, not just sports-nutrition literature.

### 4. Safety

- What is creatine's established safety profile at typical maintenance and loading doses, across short-term and long-term (multi-year) studies?
- Is there an established Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) from any authoritative body (NIH ODS, EFSA, etc.)? If none exists, say so explicitly and explain why (e.g., because no dose-dependent harm has been established, not because it hasn't been studied).
- Address the "creatine harms the kidneys" claim directly: what does the evidence actually show in people with normal kidney function vs. people with pre-existing kidney disease? Is this a persistent myth, a real caution for a specific population, or something in between?
- Any known contraindications, drug interactions, or populations that should be more cautious (e.g., pregnancy, pre-existing renal impairment)?
- What would a scientifically defensible, non-alarmist safety statement look like for a general consumer audience?

### 5. Worldwide benchmark products

I need to know the best-in-class creatine supplement products sold globally, so I can compare what's on Israeli shelves against a worldwide standard (not just against other Israeli products). For each region below, please identify 2-4 well-regarded creatine products (brand + specific product name), and for each one report: the creatine form used, the dose per serving, price (local currency and/or USD), serving count per container, and anything notable about third-party testing/certification (e.g., NSF Certified for Sport, Informed-Sport) or lack thereof.

- United States
- Canada
- Australia
- European Union (any major market — Germany, UK if available, etc.)
- Israel (Israeli-market creatine products, if you have visibility into that market; if not, say so — don't guess)

### 6. Label-honesty / dose-adequacy criteria

- What distinguishes an honestly labeled creatine product from a "fairy-dusted" one (an ingredient listed but present in a trivial, non-functional amount)?
- How common is it for creatine to be hidden inside a "proprietary blend" or "performance matrix" without a per-ingredient dose disclosed? Is this a known industry practice for creatine specifically, or more of a pre-workout/multi-ingredient-stack problem?
- What per-serving disclosure standard would let a consumer actually verify they're getting an effective dose from a label alone?

### 7. Creatine in functional dairy / protein drinks

- Creatine is increasingly appearing as an added ingredient in protein-fortified dairy drinks and yogurts (for example, products in the Tnuva GO line in Israel, and comparable products internationally). What creatine doses are typically used in this product category when it does appear, based on any products or formulations you can find (international examples are fine, not just Israeli)?
- Do the doses typically seen in these functional-dairy products reach the effective daily range you identified in section 2, or do they tend to be sub-clinical/token amounts?
- Are there known co-formulation issues specific to adding creatine to a dairy or protein-drink matrix (e.g., interactions with protein, sugars, pH adjustments used in dairy beverages, pasteurization/UHT heat exposure) beyond the general stability question in section 3?

## What to bring back, and in what format

Please structure your final answer as a report with these sections, in this order: (1) Efficacy by claim, with an evidence-strength label per claim (Strong / Moderate / Weak / Insufficient) and citations; (2) Dose ranges by goal; (3) Forms and bioavailability, including the dairy-stability question; (4) Safety, including the UL question and the kidney-myth verdict; (5) Worldwide benchmark product table (region, brand, product, form, dose/serving, price, certification); (6) Label-honesty criteria; (7) Creatine-in-dairy findings. For every factual claim, put the citation immediately next to it (not just in a bibliography at the end) so it's traceable. Flag anywhere you were unable to find a real source or had to rely on general knowledge rather than a specific study — I need to know the difference between "well-established" and "plausible but unverified."

## For Bari's internal use (not part of the copy-paste prompt)

**What we will do with the returned results:**

1. **Verify every citation before it touches anything consumer-facing.** No PMID/DOI is trusted on sight — this is the standing citation-fabrication gate. Research/Nutrition will spot-check identifiers against PubMed/CrossRef before any claim is used.
2. **Reconcile against the existing ratified dossier.** Bari already has a Nutrition-cosigned creatine evidence dossier (`03_operations/supplement_engine/proto_v0/evidence_dossiers/creatine_monohydrate.yaml`) rating strength/lean-mass as **Strong** and fat-loss as **Insufficient**, with a 3-5 g/day effective dose range and no established UL. The returned research either corroborates this (no change) or surfaces a genuine conflict (routed back through D6/D7 before anything moves). New claim areas not yet in the dossier — cognitive function, recovery, dairy-matrix stability — get evaluated for addition to the dossier under the same evidence-tier discipline.
3. **Feed the Supplement Intelligence Engine (SIE) dose-adequacy dossier**, specifically: any refinement to the effective-dose range (section 2), form-superiority findings (section 3), and the safety/UL verdict (section 4) are candidate updates to the dossier's `effective_dose`, `forms`, and `safety` fields — each still requires Nutrition review before the dossier changes, and dossier changes alone never move a live score without a separate D6/D7 pass.
4. **Slot the worldwide-benchmark table (section 5) into the comparison-page build**, the same way the magnesium comparison ranked Israeli products against a worldwide-best using a Promise-Delivery-Ratio-style lens (delivered dose vs. benchmark floor) plus price-value. Product Agent and Nutrition jointly review before any specific benchmark product name ships in consumer copy.
5. **Use the dairy-stability and functional-dairy findings (sections 3 and 7) as direct input to the `functional_dose_ingredient_ruling_v1.md` dose-adequacy check** already defined for creatine-in-dairy products (Tnuva GO and similar) — this is the annotation-only lane already ruled on; the new research fills the evidence gap that ruling flagged as open (collagen/other ingredients still pending, but creatine's own dairy-stability question was exactly this kind of gap).
6. **Nothing from this research moves a published score by itself.** Any scoring-relevant finding still routes through the standard evidence-registry (EV-###) → D6 proposal → D7 co-sign (Nutrition + Product) path before it can affect a grade. This research pass is evidence-gathering only.

## What was deliberately scoped out of the prompt

Creatine timing (pre- vs. post-workout) and creatine-caffeine interaction research were left out — they're real literature areas but not decision-relevant to a comparison page or an explainer blog focused on "does this product deliver an effective, honestly labeled dose safely," so including them would have diluted the prompt's focus without changing anything Bari would build differently.
