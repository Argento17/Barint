# Sweetener Guide — Concept v1

## The finding that decides the guide

The public sweetener conversation and the Israeli shelf are **about different molecules.**

What people argue about: aspartame, saccharin, allulose, and lately erythritol.
What is actually in the products Bari scored: **maltitol, sucralose, sorbitol, stevia.**

Measured over 1,922 distinct products: aspartame **0**, saccharin **0**, allulose **0**.
Maltitol **57**, sucralose **54**, sorbitol **29**, stevia **18**, acesulfame-K **15**,
erythritol **11**, xylitol **6**.

The most common sweetener on the shelf, **maltitol**, is the one nobody writes guides about.
Erythritol, which carries the loudest headline, appears in 11 products.

## Scope (owner ruling, 2026-07-10) — this is the binding definition

The guide has three jobs and no fourth:

1. **Tell people what they are consuming.** Name the sweetener, in Hebrew, as it appears on the label.
2. **Tell them where to find it.** Which product categories it actually shows up in.
3. **Give the evidence, with its caveats.** For interest and public knowledge.

It does not advise. It does not rank. It does not tell anyone what to eat or how worried to be. It
describes the shelf and reports the state of the evidence honestly, including where the evidence is
weak, contested, or absent. Nothing here is being re-invented — the sweeteners are already scored,
the evidence is already filed, and the shelf has already been measured. The guide surfaces what
exists.

This scope is what makes the guide publishable. A descriptive guide makes no health claim, so it
clears DEC-006 and the no-health-claims rule by construction rather than by careful wording.

## What the guide is

**A correction of the shelf, not a verdict on molecules.**

Bari's brand anchor is clarity, and independence is what makes it credible. This guide is the
purest expression of that anchor available: we can say what is actually in the food, in order of
how often it appears, because we read the labels. Nobody writing about sweeteners in Hebrew has
done that.

The guide answers the question people actually have when they eat a protein bar and feel unwell,
which is not "will this give me cancer" but "what is this and why did it do that." That question
has a real, evidence-backed, non-alarmist answer: polyols, dose, and osmotic load (EV-005).

## What the guide is NOT

- Not "how worried should I be about sweetener X." That is alarm framing, prohibited by DEC-006,
  and it is a health-outcome claim Bari does not make.
- Not organized by internal evidence tiers. The words `contested`, `likely-neutral`, and
  `confirmed-negative` are data-state and never reach a consumer (owner ruling 2026-07-08).
- Not a safe/unsafe list. The register is "what the evidence shows and does not show."
- Not a scoring change. Sweeteners are already scored; this guide moves nothing.

## Structure (proposed)

1. **What is actually on the shelf.** Order by prevalence. Establishes authority immediately and
   is the one thing only Bari can write.
2. **Sugar alcohols (the 85-product story).** Maltitol leads. The honest mechanism is osmotic and
   dose-dependent (EV-005). Tolerance differs between polyols; erythritol is the best absorbed and
   best tolerated, maltitol and sorbitol are not. This is the section that helps the most people.
3. **The high-intensity sweeteners (55 products).** Sucralose dominates; acesulfame-K sits almost
   entirely in yogurt. EV-017's glucose-tolerance/microbiome evidence applies to **sucralose**, with
   honest responder heterogeneity. EV-017's saccharin arm is shelf-irrelevant here.

   **New, and neither research return caught it.** EFSA re-evaluated sucralose in February 2026
   (EFSA Journal 2026;24:e9854, DOI `10.2903/j.efsa.2026.9854`, PMID `41710869`). It **confirmed the
   ADI of 15 mg/kg bw/day for currently authorised uses**, and separately **declined to extend
   sucralose to fine bakery wares**, citing uncertainty over the formation of chlorinated compounds
   when sucralose is heated for prolonged periods above 120 °C. Bari's corpus carries sucralose in
   baked categories (cakes_hard_cookies, cookies_coffee). This is exactly the register the guide
   wants: a current regulator confirming ordinary use while declining a heated use, stated plainly,
   with no advice attached. Verify the 120 °C figure against the primary opinion before publication.
4. **Plant-derived (20 products).** Stevia and monk fruit. Smallest presence; least contested.
5. **The erythritol correction** (rewritten 2026-07-10 against the research returns).
   The headline studies measured **fasting plasma** erythritol and xylitol, which under metabolic
   stress reflect **endogenous synthesis via the pentose phosphate pathway** rather than dietary
   intake. Three independent lines converge: a five-week clinical intake of either polyol produced
   no change in vascular endothelial function, fat distribution, or glucose tolerance; a bidirectional
   **Mendelian randomisation study found no supportive evidence of a causal association** between
   circulating erythritol and cardiometabolic disease (Khafagy, Paterson & Dash, *Diabetes*
   2024;73(2):325–331, DOI `10.2337/db23-0330`, PMID `37939167`); and FDA and EFSA left their
   assessments unchanged.
   (Citation hygiene: the research return cited this MR study as `10.2337/db23-0800`, which does not
   resolve, alongside an unrelated commentary. The study is real and the finding is as stated; the
   identifiers were wrong. Use the two above.)
   **Do not overstate the reassurance either.** There *is* an ingestion-intervention study showing
   raised platelet reactivity after erythritol (ATVB 2024, DOI `10.1161/ATVBAHA.124.321019`) — it is
   not observational-only. It used a high bolus dose that exceeds ordinary consumer exposure.
   The honest sentence is about **dose and the difference between plasma level and diet**, and it is
   neither "safe" nor "dangerous." This section is Bari being a clarity brand in public.

5b. **The label hook (new, and strong).** Israeli labels already carry a **mandatory polyol laxative
   warning** on sugar-alcohol products. Consumers physically see this text and do not know what it
   means. The guide explains a warning already in their hand. This is label-derivable, requires no
   new data, and is the most concrete consumer utility available here.
6. **What is not in your food.** Aspartame, saccharin, allulose: zero products. Disarming, true,
   and unavailable to any competitor who has not counted.
7. **What we cannot tell you.** Per-product sweetener *dose* is rarely on a label; individual
   tolerance genuinely varies.

## The editorial hazard to resolve at the gates

Sections 1 and 6 derive their power from **counts of Bari's own corpus**. Stating "we scored 1,922
products and found zero aspartame" is provenance prose, and the standing owner ruling forbids
consumer copy that narrates data-state. The insight is real and worth keeping; the phrasing is not
obviously permissible.

This is a genuine editorial question, not a technicality. It must be resolved by the Content Agent
and the Adversarial QA / Red-Team gate **before** any draft reaches the owner — not decided
inline. Framing the finding as a claim about *the food* ("aspartame is not used in these products")
rather than about *Bari's data* is the likely resolution, but that call belongs to the gates.
