# Sweetener Guide — Verified Fact Base

## 1. The Israeli label law (the guide's anchor) — PRIMARY VERIFIED

**Regulation:** תקנות הגנה על בריאות הציבור (מזון) (סימון מזון המכיל ממתיק מסוגים מסוימים), **תשע״ט-2018**
Verified against Nevo (`nevo.co.il/law_html/law01/501_965.htm`) and Hebrew Wikisource, which agree verbatim.

> **Note the corrections.** The research return called this `תשע״ח-2018` and
> `תקנות בריאות הציבור`. Both are wrong. It is **תשע״ט** and **תקנות הגנה על בריאות הציבור**.

- **Effective:** 1 January 2021 (יום י״ז בטבת התשפ״א).
- **Definition of רב-כוהליים (polyols):** "כוהלים המכילים יותר משתי קבוצות הידרוקסיל במולקולה"
  (alcohols containing more than two hydroxyl groups per molecule).
  → Erythritol has four hydroxyl groups, so it **is** a polyol under this definition and is **not exempt**.
- **Food threshold:** "רב-כוהליים שהוספו למזון שכמותם מהווה מעל 10 אחוזים ממשקל המזון"
  (added polyols exceeding **10% of the weight of the food**).
- **The mandated warning, verbatim:**
  > **"צריכה מופרזת עלולה לגרום לפעילות מעיים מוגברת"**
  (Excessive consumption may cause increased intestinal activity.)
- **Tabletop sweeteners (ממתיק שולחני):** the warning applies at **any quantity** of polyols. No 10% threshold.
- **Aspartame warning, verbatim:** "מכיל אספרטיים (מקור של פנילאלנין)".

**Spelling ruling.** The statute writes aspartame as **אספרטיים** in the mandated warning. On an
ingredient list the common declaration is **אספרטם**. Both are correct **in their own context**.
Do not "fix" the statutory string when quoting the warning. (This corrects an instruction Bari gave
its researchers twice; they were right and we were wrong.)

- **Polyol grams** are declared in the nutrition panel under carbohydrates ("מתוכן רב-כוהליים").
  The >10% rule governs only the extra warning sentence. *(Reported by research; NOT independently
  primary-verified. Confirm before publishing as a claim about the panel.)*

> ### ⚠️ The 10% threshold is CATEGORICAL, not merely quantitative (added 2026-07-10)
>
> The regulation reads "**רב-כוהליים** שהוספו למזון שכמותם מהווה מעל 10 אחוזים ממשקל המזון".
> The threshold measures the weight of **added polyols**. It has no application whatsoever to a
> sweetener that is not a polyol.
>
> **Sucralose, acesulfame-K, saccharin, aspartame and steviol glycosides can never trigger the polyol
> warning at any quantity.** Not because they are used in small amounts. Because the rule does not
> reach them at all.
>
> A v4 draft of the guide wrote that high-intensity sweeteners are used in quantities "far from
> approaching the 10% threshold, **and therefore** the product carries no polyol warning." That
> "therefore" is false, and it implies that a large enough dose of sucralose would trip the warning.
> It would not.
>
> **The true statement** (and it still carries the narrative): a manufacturer who sweetens with a
> high-intensity sweetener does not need a bulk polyol at all, and the polyol warning applies only to
> polyols. The escape from the warning is the escape from the whole category.
>
> **Corollary, equally binding:** we do **not** know which products exceed 10% by weight. The warning
> text appears in **zero** scraped records (see TASK-572). Copy may state that the warning appears
> when added polyols exceed 10% of the food's weight. Copy may **not** state or imply that any
> particular product, category, or shelf actually carries it.
>
> ### This error has now occurred TWICE. Read before writing any bridge sentence.
>
> - **v4, §3:** high-intensity sweeteners are used "far from the 10% threshold, **and therefore**
>   carry no warning." Corrected.
> - **v5, §4:** plant-derived sweeteners sweeten "in a tiny quantity **that stays far below the
>   warning threshold**," reached via the bridge phrase "the same logic of tiny quantity."
>   Corrected. The bridge itself propagated the error: it recast a **categorical** rule as a
>   **quantitative** one, and the next section inherited the false version.
>
> **Rule for writers.** Any sentence that places a **non-polyol** sweetener (sucralose,
> acesulfame-K, saccharin, aspartame, steviol glycosides, monk fruit, allulose) in any relation
> whatsoever to the 10% threshold — below it, near it, far from it — **is wrong by construction.**
> The threshold does not measure them. The correct connective is never "the same logic of small
> quantity." It is "the rule reaches only polyols."
>
> **Rule for reviewers.** A narrative bridge ("therefore", "the same logic", "which is why") is where
> an unsourced inference hides. Check the bridge, not only the sentence it connects. Three of this
> guide's four false claims lived in a bridge, and each one quoted the regulation correctly.

## 3. Sucralose — the finding both research returns missed

EFSA's February 2026 re-evaluation (same DOI/PMID above) did **two** things:

1. **Confirmed the ADI of 15 mg/kg bw/day for currently authorised uses.** Exposure estimates did not
   exceed the ADI; no safety concern at reported uses and use levels.
2. **Declined to extend sucralose to fine bakery wares.** Verbatim: *"the Panel could not conclude on
   the safety of the proposed extension of use of E 955 in this FC"* due to *"identified uncertainties
   regarding the potential formation of chlorinated compounds under the wide range of baking processes."*

**PRIMARY VERIFIED (PMC12911467, the opinion's full text).** Verbatim mechanism:

> *"when heated to temperatures of approx. **120°C–250°C**, sucralose (E 955) is dechlorinated,
> possibly leading to the generation of chlorinated organic compounds exhibiting potential health
> risk (e.g. polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins (PCDDs), dibenzofurans (PCDFs) or chloropropanols)."*

ADI confirmation, verbatim: *"there is no need to revise the current ADI of 15 mg/kg bw per day of
sucralose (E 955)"*, and *"the exposure estimates considering the currently authorised uses did not
exceed the ADI."*

Sucralose is the **second most common sweetener on Bari's shelf (54 products)** and appears in baked
categories (`cakes_hard_cookies`, `cookies_coffee`).

> ### ⛔ DO NOT PUBLISH THIS IN THE GUIDE — corrected 2026-07-10 after Red-Team RT-1 (CRITICAL)
>
> An earlier version of this section instructed Content to state both halves and added: *"no implied
> warning about any product on the shelf — the declined extension concerns an application that was not
> authorised."* **That reassurance is indefensible and is withdrawn.**
>
> The hazard EFSA describes is a **heat-driven chemical mechanism** (dechlorination at 120–250 °C),
> which is not confined to the one unauthorised food category. Bari's own corpus carries sucralose in
> baked products, and baking reaches those temperatures. Placing "sucralose is in baked goods" beside
> "heating sucralose may form chlorinated compounds" plants an alarm about authorised, on-shelf
> products that the following sentence cannot defuse. A hostile nutrition scientist rebuts it in one
> line. It breaches DEC-006 (alarm framing) and the no-health-claims rule, in both directions at once:
> the hazard is implied, and the reassurance is a safety claim Bari cannot substantiate.
>
> **Ruling: the heated/chlorinated-compound material is OUT of the consumer guide.** The guide may
> state that EFSA re-evaluated sucralose in February 2026 and confirmed the ADI of 15 mg/kg bw/day for
> currently authorised uses. It stops there. The declined bakery extension is regulatory news about an
> application, and it does not belong in a descriptive shelf guide.
>
> **This does not make the finding go away.** Whether sucralose is authorised in baked goods under
> *Israeli* law, and whether EFSA's dechlorination finding bears on any product Bari has scored, is a
> real open question with possible D4/EV consequences. It is routed to Nutrition + Research as separate
> work. It is **not** resolved by omitting it from a guide, and it must not be published anywhere until
> that question is answered.

## 4. Maltitol — the shelf leader (57 products)

**Primary source for the glycemic values:** Livesey G. *Health potential of polyols as sugar
replacers, with emphasis on low glycaemic properties.* **Nutr Res Rev 2003;16(2):163–191.
DOI `10.1079/nrr200371`, PMID `19087388`.** (Verified; both identifiers resolve.)

- Caloric value ≈ **2.1–2.4 kcal/g** (FDA uses 2.1; EU polyol labelling uses 2.4), about half of sucrose's 4.
- **Glycemic index = 35** (Livesey), against **sucrose = 65**. Low, but distinctly non-zero.
  > **Correction:** the research return gave 36 and 68. Livesey's peer-reviewed table gives **35 and 65**.
  > Use Livesey's figures, or say "roughly half of sugar's" and cite him.
- Livesey's full GI table, for the polyols on our shelf: **erythritol 0 · sorbitol 9 · isomalt 9 ·
  xylitol 13 · maltitol 35**. Maltitol is the **only** polyol on the shelf in the low-GI band rather
  than the very-low band. That single fact carries the whole section.
- **Osmotic/laxative effects from roughly 20 g as a single dose**, or 30–50 g/day.
  **STILL NOT PRIMARY-VERIFIED.** Corroborated across multiple secondary sources and consistent with
  EV-005's 10 g/serving clinical trigger. Livesey (above) is the likely primary and covers polyol
  laxation thresholds; a candidate RCT is PMC4013556 (n=36, maltitol dairy desserts).
  **Attach a resolving DOI/PMID to the 20 g figure, or omit the number and describe the effect
  qualitatively.** Do not publish it on secondary sourcing.
- Partially absorbed in the small intestine; the unabsorbed fraction is fermented in the colon,
  producing gas and drawing water osmotically.
- **Erythritol is better tolerated** (near-complete absorption, ~0.2 kcal/g; mild symptoms typically
  only around 35–50 g). *(Tolerance range not primary-verified; same rule applies.)*

## 5. Erythritol and xylitol — explain, do not alarm

- The headline studies measured **fasting plasma** levels, which under hyperglycaemia, metabolic
  syndrome and oxidative stress substantially reflect **endogenous synthesis via the pentose
  phosphate pathway** rather than dietary intake.
- **An ingestion-intervention study exists and must not be described as observational.** Witkowski
  et al., ATVB 2024, DOI `10.1161/ATVBAHA.124.321019` — title begins *"**Ingestion** of the
  Non-Nutritive Sweetener Erythritol…"*. A ~30 g bolus raised platelet reactivity.
  **It was ingestion, not infusion** (the research return says "infused" twice; that is wrong, and it
  matters — infusion would bypass the gut entirely).
  The dose far exceeds ordinary consumption.
- **Mendelian randomisation found no supportive evidence of causation:** Khafagy R, Paterson AD,
  Dash S. *Erythritol as a Potential Causal Contributor to Cardiometabolic Disease: A Mendelian
  Randomization Study.* **Diabetes 2024;73(2):325–331. DOI `10.2337/db23-0330`, PMID `37939167`.**
  > The research return cited this as `10.2337/db23-0800` (**does not resolve**) paired with
  > `10.5114/hpc.2024.140951`, which is a different paper ("Hidden Risks of Xylitol and Erythritol:
  > A Call for Further Research"). The study is real; the identifiers were wrong. Use the two above.
- Xylitol: Witkowski et al., *Eur Heart J* 2024, DOI `10.1093/eurheartj/ehae244` (verified).
  The critique is `10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf058` (verified) — cite it **as a critique**, never as the
  source of the association.
- **FDA and EFSA have not changed their erythritol assessments.**

> ### ⚠️ PRESENCE IS NOT FUNCTION — corrected 2026-07-10. The fifth false inference, and it was mine.
>
> The scan counted **ingredient tokens**, and the fact base then called the result "sweeteners." That
> inference is false. A polyol can be present as a **humectant** (moisture agent) rather than as a
> sweetener.
>
> **Measured on the sorbitol-bearing products (n=19):**
> - **13 (68%)** declare sorbitol under **`חומרי הלחה`** (humectants), not under `ממתיקים`.
> - **17 (89%)** also list **`סוכר`** (sugar) as an ingredient.
> - Only **4 (21%)** use the word `ממתיק` anywhere.
> - **11** are the clean case: sorbitol as a humectant in an ordinary **sugar-containing** cake.
>   Example: `עוגת הבית שיש אסם` — first ingredient `סוכר`, sorbitol declared under `חומרי הלחה`
>   beside glycerol.
>
> **Consequences.**
> 1. "Polyols are the largest **sweetener** group on the shelf" is **not supported**. The token count is
>    inflated by sorbitol appearing as a humectant. Maltitol and erythritol are genuinely used as
>    sweeteners here; sorbitol on this shelf mostly is not.
> 2. **The warning story is unaffected.** The regulation covers `רב-כוהליים שהוספו למזון` — *added
>    polyols* — irrespective of technological function. A humectant polyol still counts toward the 10%
>    and still triggers the warning above it.
> 3. This is a **more interesting fact, not a weaker one**: a shopper meets sorbitol in an ordinary
>    sugary chocolate cake, where it keeps the crumb soft. Polyols are not confined to "sugar-free."
>
> **Rule.** Never infer a sweetener's *role* from the presence of its name. Read the functional group it
> is declared under (`ממתיקים` / `חומרי הלחה` / `מייצבים`). The ingredient list states the function.
> Our scan did not read it, and the fact base asserted it anyway.

> ### ⛔ No plant-sweetener product on this shelf is polyol-free (added 2026-07-10, RT-2)
>
> The orchestrator described `גרנולה תותים ללת"ס` to the writer as a *"clean single-sweetener
> anchor"* for stevia, without reading its label. Its label declares **erythritol**, a polyol.
> So does `עוגיות קיטו שקד לוז`. §4 of the guide argues that plant-derived sweeteners are not
> polyols and the law does not reach them, and then illustrates it with two products that both
> contain a polyol the law **does** reach.
>
> Measured over every product whose ingredient list carries a `ממתיקים(...)` declaration naming
> steviol glycosides or monk fruit: **10 products, 0 of them polyol-free.** Steviol glycosides and
> mogrosides sweeten at very small weights, so something must supply the bulk, and on this shelf the
> bulk is a polyol. (This count is for internal reasoning. It is a corpus statistic and must never
> appear in consumer copy.)
>
> **Consequence for the copy.** The claim "the law does not reach plant-derived sweeteners" is true
> **of the sweetener** and false if a reader hears it **of the product**. It cannot be fixed by
> swapping in a cleaner product, because there is none. It must be fixed in the sentence: scope the
> statement to the substance, and state the named product's label honestly, including the erythritol.
> Never state or imply whether any product does or does not carry the statutory warning.

> ### ⛔ A product name is a VERBATIM string, not a tidy paraphrase (added 2026-07-10)
>
> This table previously listed `דנונה פרו לל״ס וניל` and `אול אין דאבל שוקולד`. **Neither name exists
> in the corpus.** They were produced by cleaning up truncated retail strings into names that read
> plausibly. The first reached the consumer band in v8 and v9 before it was caught. Retail label
> strings are ugly (`דנונה פרו לל"ס וניל עוג.`, doubled spaces, trailing periods) and the ugliness is
> the evidence that they were not invented.
>
> This is the **seventh** false inference in this guide and it has the same shape as the other six:
> a verified particular (this product exists and declares acesulfame-K) joined to an unverified
> assertion (that it is *called* this).
>
> Compounding it: `דנונה פרו 20 גר וניל 0%` is a **different** product that contains `סוכר לבן` and
> **no** acesulfame-K. A shopper sent to look for "the vanilla Danone Pro" can pick up the sugared tub.
> A near-miss product name is not a cosmetic error; it misdirects the reader at the shelf.
>
> **Rule for writers and reviewers:** every product name printed in consumer copy must be an exact
> string match against `ingredients_raw`'s record. Never normalise quotes, spacing, or abbreviations
> for readability. If a real name is too ugly to print, choose a different real product. Never
> beautify one.
>
> `אול אין דאבל שוקולד` is struck from this table; it never entered the copy. It may return only
> after its exact corpus name is confirmed.

**The chocolate bar is the strongest example in the whole guide.** One dark chocolate bar declares

## 9. What we cannot say (retain in the guide)

- No empirical evidence that Israeli consumers confuse stevia, sucralose, and erythritol.
- No data showing post-tax-repeal reformulation reversal.
- No permitted sweetener is shown to be harmful at normal intake.
- Cardiovascular findings for erythritol and xylitol do not establish that eating these at
  food-label levels causes thrombosis.
- Per-product sweetener **dose** is not readable from a label; only presence and identity are.
