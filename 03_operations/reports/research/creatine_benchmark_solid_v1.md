## 4. What best-in-class looks like (reference paragraph)

Across this set, the pattern that separates a strong creatine product from a
mediocre one is consistent and evidence-anchored: **monohydrate form** (the only
form with the weight of human evidence behind it — HCl, buffered, and other
"premium" forms carry no demonstrated advantage, per
`creatine_evidence_cosign_v1.md` row 14, Strong consensus); **a labeled serving of
at least 3 g, ideally the studied-typical 5 g**, stated as an exact gram figure
rather than a teaspoon/percentage/blend that hides the real dose; and **third-party
batch certification verified in the certifier's own directory** — not just a badge
image on the brand's packaging, since badge claims and directory listings
demonstrably diverge in this dataset (Naked Nutrition's own page states "NSF
certified" but no matching nsfsport.com listing surfaced this pass; a Vitacost
listing still uses a stale "Momentous Creapure" title the brand's own page
contradicts). On **price-value**, the directory-confirmed NSF products in this set
cluster in a real, computable $0.16–$0.27-per-3g-dose band (BPN, Momentous, Naked
Nutrition, Thorne) — a legitimate basis for a "you don't have to pay a premium for
verified quality" finding, while the lowest-dose product in the set (BioSteel
Canada, 2.5 g/scoop) shows that a NSF-certified product can still under-deliver on
dose at its labeled default serving, which is a genuinely different axis from
certification and should never be conflated with it in comparison-page copy.
