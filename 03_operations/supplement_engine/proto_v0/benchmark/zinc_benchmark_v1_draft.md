# Zinc Worldwide Benchmark - v1 draft (research-only)

> Evidence-only benchmark for oral zinc supplements. No scoring, no code, no Open Food Facts. All dose values refer to elemental zinc unless explicitly stated as compound mass.

## 1. Standing Caveats
1. **Elemental zinc governs.** A label that says "zinc gluconate 50 mg" is not the same as "zinc 50 mg as zinc gluconate"; zinc gluconate is about 14.3% elemental zinc by molecular-weight calculation (zinc 65.38 g/mol; zinc gluconate 455.68 g/mol; PubChem: https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc and https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc-gluconate).
2. **The benchmark is not a cure benchmark.** US RDA adequacy is 11 mg/day for adult men and 8 mg/day for adult women, while the adult UL is 40 mg/day (IOM/NAM DRI tables: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab3/ and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab9/). Supplements above the RDA can be rational in deficiency or short-course cold-lozenge use, but routine chronic high-dose use is a different risk category.
3. **Authority divergence is real.** EFSA dietary requirements are phytate-dependent: adult male PRI ranges about 9.4-16.3 mg/day and adult female PRI ranges about 7.5-12.7 mg/day across phytate intakes (EFSA DRV zinc opinion: https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2014.3844). EU historical adult UL is 25 mg/day from the Scientific Committee on Food, while the US/NAM adult UL is 40 mg/day (SCF opinion: https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-12/sci-com_scf_out177_en.pdf; NAM/IOM: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK222310/).
4. **Form marketing outruns evidence.** Picolinate, citrate, gluconate, acetate, sulfate, oxide, and chelates are not interchangeable, but the human comparative evidence base is smaller than the marketing claims suggest. Zinc oxide is dense at about 80.3% elemental zinc by molecular-weight calculation (zinc oxide 81.38 g/mol; zinc 65.38 g/mol; PubChem: https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc-oxide and https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc), but density does not equal absorption.
5. **Price is within-region only.** Prices are retailer snapshots captured 2026-06-20 from the cited retailer pages/search pages; they are not compared across countries.

## 3. Form Ranking + Honest-Label Bar

### Form ranking by direct human bioavailability evidence and practical benchmark value
1. **Soluble organic salts with direct use in trials: acetate and gluconate lozenges for colds.** These are not automatically "best daily forms," but they are the best reference forms for the cold-lozenge claim because Cochrane reports most lozenge studies used gluconate and acetate, with lozenge doses 45-276 mg/day (https://www.cochrane.org/CD014914/ARI_zinc-preventing-and-treating-common-cold).
2. **Citrate / gluconate / picolinate for daily supplements.** Human absorption literature supports attention to solubility and meal context; citrate and amino acids can improve absorption conditions, and phytate strongly inhibits absorption (Lonnerdal review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10801947/). Picolinate has a strong marketing presence, but the head-to-head evidence is not strong enough to crown it universally superior.
3. **Sulfate / acetate capsules or tablets.** Zinc sulfate monohydrate is about 36.4% elemental zinc and zinc sulfate heptahydrate is about 22.7% elemental zinc by molecular-weight calculation (PubChem zinc sulfate: https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc-sulfate; zinc: https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc). Sulfate is clinically familiar but often less gentle.
4. **Bisglycinate / amino-acid chelates / methionine.** These may be tolerability-forward, but premium claims often outrun direct comparative human evidence. Bisglycinate elemental fraction varies by chelate and hydration state; a simple zinc bisglycinate anhydrous formula is about 28.9% elemental by formula mass, but commercial chelates may declare 14-20% depending ligand/hydrate/excipient standardization (PubChem zinc bisglycinate: https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc-bis_glycinate; Albion/TRAACS-style products should still declare elemental zinc per serving).
5. **Oxide.** Zinc oxide is about 80.3% elemental zinc by molecular-weight calculation (PubChem zinc oxide: https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/Zinc-oxide), but high elemental density does not solve absorption; use it as a low-cost adequacy ingredient, not as a premium bioavailability claim.

### Honest-label bar
1. Label states **elemental zinc per serving** in mg, not only compound mass; adult RDA context is 8-11 mg/day and adult US UL is 40 mg/day (NAM/IOM: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab3/ and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab9/).
2. Label names the zinc species: picolinate, citrate, gluconate, acetate, sulfate, oxide, bisglycinate, etc.; "zinc" alone is less transparent.
3. Claim matches evidence: immune/skin/fertility/cognition/bone claims are normal-function nutrient claims in the EU, not proof that 50 mg/day performs better than 15 mg/day (Regulation EU 432/2012: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02012R0432-20210517).
4. Products at 25-50 mg/day include a copper-balance warning or are positioned for short-term/clinician-guided use because adult ULs are 25 mg/day in the EU/SCF opinion and 40 mg/day in NAM/IOM (SCF: https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-12/sci-com_scf_out177_en.pdf; NAM/IOM: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab9/).
5. Cold-lozenge products distinguish short-course lozenge protocols from chronic capsules; Cochrane lozenge treatment doses ranged 45-276 mg/day for 4.5-21 days (https://www.cochrane.org/CD014914/ARI_zinc-preventing-and-treating-common-cold).

## 4. Safety
1. **Adult UL:** 40 mg/day elemental zinc in the US/NAM DRI tables (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab9/). The UL is not just about nausea; NAM/IOM selected endpoints include copper status and immune-related effects (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK222310/).
2. **Distinctive zinc risk:** chronic high zinc can induce copper deficiency. NIH ODS describes excess zinc causing copper deficiency, reduced immune function, and lower HDL cholesterol; symptoms of excess intake include nausea, dizziness, headaches, gastric distress, vomiting, and loss of appetite (https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/).
3. **EU caution:** SCF adult UL is 25 mg/day (https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-12/sci-com_scf_out177_en.pdf). A product at 30 mg/day is below the US 40 mg/day UL but above the EU/SCF 25 mg/day adult UL.
4. **Cold lozenges are a special case:** Cochrane reports treatment doses up to 276 mg/day and increased non-serious adverse events with RR 1.34 (https://www.cochrane.org/CD014914/ARI_zinc-preventing-and-treating-common-cold). That makes lozenges a short-course therapeutic-style benchmark, not a daily multi-month benchmark.
5. **Interactions:** zinc can interfere with absorption of some antibiotics and penicillamine, and high supplemental zinc can interfere with copper; NIH ODS lists these interactions (https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/).

## 7. Divergences & Limitations
1. **RDA/PRI divergence:** US/NAM adult RDA is 11 mg/day for men and 8 mg/day for women (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab3/). EFSA adult PRI varies with phytate intake, about 9.4-16.3 mg/day for men and 7.5-12.7 mg/day for women (https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2014.3844). WHO/FAO requirement estimates also vary by diet bioavailability and phytate burden (WHO/FAO vitamin and mineral requirements report: https://www.fao.org/4/y2809e/y2809e00.htm).
2. **UL divergence:** US/NAM adult UL is 40 mg/day (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545442/table/appJ_tab9/). EU/SCF adult UL is 25 mg/day (https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-12/sci-com_scf_out177_en.pdf). This matters because many real shelf products are 30-50 mg/day.
3. **Phytate matters:** zinc absorption is strongly inhibited by phytate in cereal/corn/rice staples; fermentation/germination/phytase can improve absorption (Lonnerdal: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10801947/). Therefore a universal intake target hides diet-pattern risk.
4. **Forms are under-specified.** "Zinc citrate" or "zinc bisglycinate" can hide hydration/chelate-standardization differences; label-declared elemental zinc is more reliable than back-calculation.
5. **Cold-lozenge evidence is not a daily supplement rule.** Cochrane included 34 studies and 8,526 participants, with treatment benefit uncertain and heterogeneous; treatment may shorten colds by 2.37 days but evidence certainty is low and adverse events increase (https://www.cochrane.org/CD014914/ARI_zinc-preventing-and-treating-common-cold).
6. **EU shelf is partial.** The EU product sample here is Germany-visible retailer evidence only, not a union-wide supplement registry. EU-wide product registration is fragmented by member state.
7. **Prices are volatile and regional.** All shelf prices are 2026-06-20 snapshots from the cited retailer search/product pages and are not comparable across currencies or countries.
8. **Unverifiable items are marked.** Where a search page exposed a product title and price but not the exact zinc species or elemental basis, the field is marked "not found" rather than inferred.
